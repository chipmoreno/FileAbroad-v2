Form 8833 is the IRS's disclosure form for treaty-based tax positions. If you are claiming a benefit under one of the 60+ US tax treaties — whether it's reduced withholding on dividends, an exemption for pension income, or a tie-breaker rule to establish residency — you may need to disclose that position on Form 8833. The penalty for failing to disclose is $1,000 per position, even if your treaty claim is perfectly valid. This guide explains when Form 8833 is required, when it is not, and how to file it correctly.

## What Is a Treaty-Based Return Position?

A treaty-based return position is any tax treatment that:
1. Is contrary to the default rules of the Internal Revenue Code
2. Is based on a provision of a US income tax treaty

In plain English: if the Internal Revenue Code says one thing, but a tax treaty says something different, and you follow the treaty, you have taken a treaty-based return position.

### Examples of Treaty Positions

| Situation | Default IRC Rule | Treaty Position | Form 8833 Required? |
|-----------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------------|
| UK resident with US dividends | 30% withholding | 15% under US-UK treaty | Usually yes |
| German professor working in US | Taxable in US | Exempt under teacher article | Yes |
| Dual resident (US/Canada) | Taxed as US resident | Tie-breaker makes Canadian resident | Yes |
| French pension income | Taxable in US | Exempt under pension article | Usually yes |
| Portfolio interest to non-resident | 30% withholding | Exempt under portfolio interest rules | No (exception) |

## Who Must File Form 8833?

You must file Form 8833 if you claim a treaty benefit that overrides the Internal Revenue Code, unless an exception applies.

### Common Situations Requiring Form 8833

1. **Tie-breaker rules:** You are a dual resident (citizen of one country, resident of another) and you use the treaty's tie-breaker rules to establish residency in the non-US country
2. **Exempt income:** You claim an exemption from US tax for income that would otherwise be taxable (teacher income, student income, certain pension income)
3. **Reduced rates:** You claim a reduced tax rate on dividends, interest, or royalties
4. **Permanent establishment:** You claim that your foreign business has no US permanent establishment and therefore US tax does not apply
5. **Non-discrimination:** You claim that the US cannot tax you more heavily than a US citizen in the same circumstances

### Exceptions: When Form 8833 Is NOT Required

The IRS has created several exceptions where disclosure is not required:

1. **W-8BEN claims:** If you claim treaty benefits on a Form W-8BEN provided to a withholding agent, no Form 8833 is required on your tax return
2. **Portfolio interest:** Exemption from withholding on portfolio interest does not require Form 8833
3. **Certain business profits:** If you claim that business profits are not taxable because there is no permanent establishment, disclosure may not be required if the treaty article is covered by a Revenue Procedure exception
4. **Social Security:** Benefits under the Social Security totalization articles generally do not require Form 8833
5. **IRS-published exceptions:** The IRS periodically publishes Revenue Procedures listing treaty articles that do not require disclosure

**Important:** The exceptions are narrow. If you are unsure whether an exception applies, file Form 8833 anyway. The $1,000 penalty for non-disclosure is far worse than the minor inconvenience of filing the form.

## How to File Form 8833

### Basic Information

- Attach Form 8833 to your Form 1040 (or 1040-NR for non-resident aliens)
- File by the due date of your return (April 15, June 15 for expats, or October 15 with extension)
- A separate Form 8833 is required for each treaty position

### Required Information

**Part I:** Taxpayer identification — name, address, TIN, treaty country

**Part II:** Treaty position disclosure:
- Treaty and article number (e.g., "US-UK Treaty, Article 17")
- Internal Revenue Code provision overridden
- Description of the facts supporting the position
- Explanation of the treaty-based return position

**Part III:** List of all Forms 8833 filed for the year

### Example Disclosure

> **Treaty:** US-United Kingdom Income Tax Convention
> **Article:** Article 17 (Pensions)
> **IRC provision overridden:** IRC Section 61 (gross income includes all income)
> **Facts:** Taxpayer is a US citizen resident in the UK who receives a UK state pension. The UK taxes the pension.
> **Position:** Under Article 17, the UK state pension is taxable only in the UK and is exempt from US tax.

## Common Treaty Positions for Expats

### US-UK Treaty Positions

- **Pensions:** UK state pensions are taxable only in the UK (Article 17)
- **Dividends:** Reduced withholding from 30% to 15% (Article 10)
- **Interest:** Reduced withholding from 30% to 0% or 10% depending on type (Article 11)
- **Teachers/Researchers:** Exempt from UK tax for up to 2 years (Article 20)

### US-Germany Treaty Positions

- **Pensions:** German pensions are generally taxable only in Germany (Article 18)
- **Government Service:** Government pensions are taxable only in the paying country (Article 19)
- **Students:** Exempt from US tax on certain income for up to 4 years (Article 20)

### US-Canada Treaty Positions

- **Pensions:** RRSP/RRIF income is taxable only in Canada if the recipient is a Canadian resident (Article 18)
- **Employment:** Employment income is taxable in the country of residence unless the work is performed in the other country (Article 14)
- **Dividends:** Reduced withholding to 15% or 5% (Article 10)

### US-Australia Treaty Positions

- **Pensions:** Australian superannuation is generally taxable only in Australia (Article 18)
- **Dividends:** Reduced withholding to 15% or 5% (Article 10)
- **Capital Gains:** Gains on real property are taxable in the country where the property is located (Article 13)

## How to Evaluate a Treaty Position

Begin with the domestic-law result. Identify the income item, taxpayer status, source, recipient, payer, and reporting form that would apply without a treaty. Then locate the specific treaty article and protocol, check the applicable limitation-on-benefits or residence provisions, and explain exactly how the treaty changes the result. “The treaty says I am exempt” is not a workpaper; the conclusion should identify the article, facts, tax year, and domestic rule being modified.

### Residence and tie-breaker claims

Residence claims require more than a foreign address. Review the taxpayer’s home, habitual abode, personal and economic relations, citizenship, immigration status, family location, work location, and the competent-authority framework in the particular treaty. A treaty tie-breaker may change residence for treaty purposes while the taxpayer remains a US citizen subject to the Internal Revenue Code. State taxation, information returns, and source rules can remain separate questions.

### Pension, employment, and investment income

Classify the payment before applying a treaty article. A pension distribution, social-security payment, salary, service fee, dividend, interest payment, rental receipt, and capital gain can have different articles and sourcing rules. Review withholding forms and payor statements, but do not let a payor’s withholding decision replace the taxpayer’s return analysis. Where the treaty allocates taxing rights to both countries, calculate the foreign tax credit or other relief separately.

### Disclosure file

Keep the treaty text, protocol, IRS technical explanation or current guidance, residency certificate, withholding forms, income statement, calculation, and a copy of the completed Form 8833. If an exception to disclosure is being used, cite the exception and explain why it fits. If the facts change in a later year—new country, new employer, new pension, marriage, or a move—retest the conclusion instead of copying the prior form.

### Source and timing questions

Treaty analysis should be coordinated with source rules and the tax year in which the income is received or accrued. A treaty may allocate a taxing right without changing the source of an item for every other purpose. A service provider working from abroad, a California resident receiving foreign pension income, and a U.S. person receiving a foreign dividend can each need a different combination of treaty article, source rule, withholding form, foreign tax credit, and information return. Write the conclusion for the actual facts rather than relying on a country label.

### Treaty position versus immigration status

A visa, residence permit, citizenship, or foreign tax number can support the factual record but does not by itself determine treaty residence. Review the treaty’s definition of resident, domestic-law residence in both countries, permanent home, habitual abode, center of vital interests, and any competent-authority procedure. A taxpayer may remain a U.S. citizen and file a Form 1040 while claiming a treaty position for a particular item. That position should be disclosed or documented under the applicable exception.

### Keep the calculation reproducible

A reviewer should be able to start with the tax return and trace the treaty position to the income statement, treaty article, exception, and Form 8833 entry. Keep a copy of the treaty version and protocol used, the taxpayer’s residence evidence, the payor documentation, the withholding calculation, the foreign tax credit workpaper, and any written advice from the tax authority. Revisit the position when the treaty changes or the facts move to a different country.

## The $1,000 Penalty

### When It Applies

The $1,000 penalty applies if:
- You claim a treaty-based return position
- You do not disclose it on Form 8833
- No exception applies

### Multiple Penalties

If you claim multiple treaty positions in the same year, each undisclosed position is a separate $1,000 penalty. For example:
- Claiming reduced dividend withholding AND pension exemption without disclosure = $2,000 penalty

### Corporate Penalty

For C corporations, the penalty is $10,000 per undisclosed position.

### Reasonable Cause

You may avoid the penalty if you can demonstrate reasonable cause for the failure to disclose. This is difficult to establish and requires documentation.
