Form 5471 is the disclosure form that strikes fear into every American business owner abroad. If you own a foreign corporation, a foreign LLC treated as a corporation, or even a significant stake in one, Form 5471 is how the IRS tracks your overseas business interests. Get it wrong, and the penalties start at $10,000 per year. This guide explains who must file, what information is required, and how to navigate the complex attribution rules.

## Who Must File Form 5471?

Form 5471 is required for "US persons" (citizens, residents, domestic corporations, partnerships, trusts, or estates) who fall into any of these categories:

### Category 1: US Shareholder of a CFC

You own 10% or more of a foreign corporation that is a Controlled Foreign Corporation (CFC). This is the most common category for expat business owners.

### Category 2: Officer or Director

You are an officer or director of a foreign corporation, and a US person acquires stock that causes them to own 10% or more.

### Category 3: Acquisition/Disposition

You acquire stock that causes you to own 10% or more, or you dispose of stock that reduces your ownership below 10%, or the foreign corporation becomes or ceases to be a CFC.

### Category 4: 10%+ Shareholder

You own 10% or more of a foreign corporation's voting stock at any time during the year.

### Category 5: CFC Shareholder

You are a US shareholder of a CFC at any time during the year and must report your pro-rata share of Subpart F income, GILTI, and other inclusions.

## What Is a Controlled Foreign Corporation (CFC)?

A CFC is a foreign corporation where:
- US shareholders collectively own **more than 50%** of the total combined voting power or total value
- Each US shareholder owns **at least 10%**

**Example:** You and two American partners each own 20% of a Costa Rican corporation. Because US persons own 60% collectively, and each owns at least 10%, the corporation is a CFC. All three of you must file Form 5471.

### Constructive Ownership Rules

The IRS looks through family relationships and entity ownership:
- Stock owned by your spouse, children, parents, or siblings is attributed to you
- Stock owned by a corporation, partnership, or trust you control is attributed to you
- These attribution rules can create CFC status unexpectedly

## What Information Does Form 5471 Require?

Form 5471 is lengthy (up to 28 schedules) and requires detailed corporate information:

### Basic Information (Schedule A)
- Identification of all US shareholders and their ownership percentages
- Stock ownership changes during the year
- Information about the foreign corporation's officers and directors

### Income Statement (Schedule C)
- Full income statement translated to US dollars
- Reconciliation to local-country financial statements
- Adjustments for US tax principles

### Balance Sheet (Schedule F)
- Opening and closing balance sheets in USD
- Reconciliation of retained earnings
- Currency translation methods

### Subpart F Income (Schedule I)
- Passive income (dividends, interest, rents, royalties)
- Foreign base company sales income
- Foreign base company services income
- Insurance income

### GILTI Calculation (Schedule I-1)
- Tested income and tested loss
- Qualified Business Asset Investment (QBAI)
- Net tested income and GILTI inclusion
- Foreign tax credits and Section 250 deduction

### Other Schedules
- Related-party transactions
- Cost sharing arrangements
- Transfer pricing documentation
- Foreign tax credit computations

## GILTI: The Tax That Changed Everything

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 introduced GILTI, which fundamentally changed how CFCs are taxed.

### What Is GILTI?

Global Intangible Low-Taxed Income is the excess of a CFC's net tested income over a 10% return on its tangible assets (QBAI). In plain English: if your foreign corporation earns more than a 10% return on its physical assets, the excess is GILTI.

### How Is GILTI Taxed?

**For corporate shareholders:**
- Include GILTI in gross income
- Claim a 50% Section 250 deduction (37.5% after 2025)
- Claim an 80% foreign tax credit on taxes paid by the CFC
- Effective US tax rate on GILTI is approximately 10.5% (13.125% after 2025)

**For individual shareholders (default):**
- Include GILTI in gross income at ordinary rates
- No Section 250 deduction
- No indirect foreign tax credit
- Effective tax rate can be 37% or higher

**Section 962 Election (individuals):**
- Allows individuals to be taxed on GILTI as if they were a corporation
- Enables the Section 250 deduction and indirect foreign tax credit
- Often reduces the effective rate to approximately 10.5-13.125%
- The election is made annually and is irrevocable for that year

## Subpart F Income

Before GILTI, Subpart F was the primary anti-deferral regime. It still applies alongside GILTI:

### Types of Subpart F Income

1. **Passive Income:** Dividends, interest, rents, royalties, annuities, excess gains from property sales
2. **Foreign Base Company Sales Income:** Income from sales where the CFC acts as a middleman between related parties
3. **Foreign Base Company Services Income:** Income from services performed for related parties outside the CFC's home country
4. **Insurance Income:** Income from insurance or reinsurance of US risks

### Current Inclusion

US shareholders must include their pro-rata share of Subpart F income in their personal return, even if the CFC makes no distributions. This prevents deferring passive income inside the foreign corporation.

## Filing Deadlines and Penalties

### When to File

Form 5471 is filed with your personal tax return (Form 1040) by:
- April 15 (for US residents)
- June 15 (automatic extension for taxpayers abroad)
- October 15 (if you file Form 4868 by June 15)

### Penalties

- **$10,000** per form per year for failure to file
- **Additional $10,000 per month** (up to $50,000) if failure continues after IRS notice
- **Criminal penalties** for willful failure to file
- **Statute of limitations suspension:** The 3-year statute of limitations on your entire tax return does not start until Form 5471 is filed

## Special Situations

### Foreign LLCs

A foreign LLC is treated differently depending on its classification:
- **Disregarded entity:** No Form 5471 (but may require Form 8858)
- **Corporation (by election or default):** Form 5471 required if 10%+ owned
- **Partnership:** Form 8865 instead of Form 5471

Many Americans abroad form foreign LLCs without considering US tax classification. The default classification for a single-member foreign LLC is disregarded, but if you elect corporate status or the LLC is automatically classified as a corporation under foreign law, Form 5471 applies.

### Foreign Startups with No Income

Even if your foreign corporation has no income or losses, you must file Form 5471 if you meet the ownership tests. The filing requirement is based on ownership, not profitability.

### Multiple CFCs

If you own multiple foreign corporations, you must file a separate Form 5471 for each one. The $10,000 penalty applies per form, so owning 3 CFCs and failing to file costs $30,000 per year.

## How to Assemble the Form 5471 Workpapers

The form is built from the corporation’s legal, accounting, ownership, and transaction records. Begin with the foreign company’s name, country of incorporation, tax identification number, functional currency, accounting period, and ownership chart. The ownership chart should show direct, indirect, and constructive ownership at the beginning and end of the year, plus any acquisitions, transfers, redemptions, or changes in voting rights. A simple percentage shown on a local share register may not capture the attribution rules used for US reporting.

### Accounting records and functional currency

Gather the trial balance, profit-and-loss statement, balance sheet, general ledger, payroll records, fixed-asset schedule, shareholder loans, and local tax return. The US return may require adjustments from local books, including different depreciation, capitalization, income-recognition, expense, and foreign-currency rules. Identify the corporation’s functional currency and document the exchange rates used. Retain the reconciliation so a reviewer can trace Form 5471 amounts back to the books rather than treating the schedules as unexplained totals.

### Separate Subpart F, tested income, and distributions

Do not assume that all CFC profit is treated alike. Analyze passive income, related-party sales and services, insurance income, previously taxed earnings and profits, tested income, tested loss, deductions, and distributions. The corporation may have current US inclusions even if it made no distribution. Conversely, a distribution may be paid from previously taxed earnings and profits and require a different treatment from a dividend out of untaxed earnings. The supporting schedule should identify the source of each amount and the shareholder’s pro-rata share.

### Transactions that deserve a second review

Flag loans to or from shareholders, related-party payments, management fees, royalties, inventory sales, asset transfers, reorganizations, changes in accounting period, and transactions involving US persons. These facts can change a filing category, create a separate schedule, or affect Subpart F, GILTI, basis, or foreign tax credit calculations. Keep signed agreements and payment records. A bank statement alone may not establish the legal purpose of an intercompany payment.

### A year-by-year remediation file

For a late or incomplete filing, create a matrix by corporation and tax year. List the ownership facts, available financial statements, prior Form 5471 schedules, missing data, income inclusions, distributions, and any penalties or notices. Then decide whether the issue is a delinquent information return, an amended income-tax return, or a broader compliance matter. This prevents a current-year filing from silently carrying forward an unresolved historical error.
