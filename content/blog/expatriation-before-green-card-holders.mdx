# Expatriation Tax Before Abandoning a Green Card

A long-term lawful permanent resident may face an expatriation-tax review when U.S. residency ends. The key date is not necessarily the day a physical card expires or the day a person leaves the United States. The immigration action, tax rules, prior years, compliance certification, assets, and Form 8854 must be coordinated.

## Establish the formal termination date

Collect the green-card history, immigration correspondence, I-407 or other abandonment record, and any final administrative or judicial order. The Form 8854 instructions describe how to report the date a long-term resident’s status ended. Do not backdate the tax result to the last day you lived in the United States without authority.

## Review long-term-resident and covered-expatriate tests

The review can include the number of years the person was treated as a lawful permanent resident, average tax liability for prior years, net worth, and five-year tax compliance. Assets may include brokerage accounts, private companies, pensions, trusts, deferred compensation, real property, and interests in foreign entities.

## Update the compliance inventory

Before the abandonment date, reconcile returns and international forms. Foreign businesses may require Form 5471 or 8858. Foreign funds may require Form 8621. Trusts, gifts, pensions, and accounts can create Forms 3520, 3520-A, 8938, or FBAR requirements. A Form 8854 certification should not be prepared from a return summary alone.

## Separate tax from immigration

Immigration counsel determines the process for abandoning permanent residence. A tax professional analyzes federal filing and expatriation consequences. Coordinate copies and dates, but keep the scopes separate.

## Turn the topic into a repeatable review workflow

Start with a complete fact inventory: filing year, U.S. status, residence and work locations, legal owner, dates, currencies, related entities, and prior filings. Gather primary documents before relying on summaries. Use the current agency instructions for the form or rule, preserve the source URL and access date, and write down any assumption that could change the result.

Then trace each fact to the return, information form, deadline, or disclosure it may affect. Reconcile the same item across the federal return, foreign return, account statements, and prior-year workpapers. If the issue involves a local-law classification, treaty position, immigration status, willfulness, or a penalty, record it as an escalation question rather than presenting a general article as a conclusion.

## Common failure modes

The most common failure is treating a general answer as a conclusion for a specific structure. A foreign label, a tax-free local result, a treaty headline, or an old filing can be a useful lead, but none is a substitute for the current form instructions and the governing documents. Another failure is collecting only year-end numbers. Many cross-border tests depend on a maximum value, a transaction during the year, a residence date, or an election that must be preserved in the return workpapers.

Do not delete ambiguous records. Keep them in a review queue with the question they create. A good queue distinguishes “document missing,” “classification uncertain,” “value needs reconstruction,” and “professional interpretation required.” That separation lets straightforward work continue while protecting the issues that should not be guessed.
