# Form 1116 Income Baskets Explained for Expats

Form 1116 uses income categories—often called baskets—to keep foreign tax credits tied to the type of foreign-source income that generated the tax. The basket is not a marketing label and it is not chosen solely by the country where you live.

## Common categories in an expat return

### General category

This often includes wages, self-employment income, and active business income. The source and allocation of the income still need to be established. A remote worker, a foreign company owner, and a person providing services from several countries may have different source questions.

### Passive category

Interest, dividends, rents, royalties, and some gains commonly fall into the passive category. Keep investment income and the related foreign tax separate from earned income. A foreign bank withholding statement is not by itself a complete Form 1116 workpaper.

### Foreign branch category

Income from a foreign branch of a U.S. business can receive separate treatment. The branch’s books, expenses, foreign taxes, and transactions with the home office are relevant.

### Section 951A category

Income included under the CFC rules can be subject to the Section 951A category. A foreign corporation’s tested income, tested loss, foreign taxes, and shareholder status must be reconciled before this category is prepared.

## Why mixing baskets creates errors

The foreign tax credit limitation is intended to stop cross-crediting. If foreign tax paid on passive income is combined with wages, the calculation may show a credit that does not correspond to the U.S. tax on the same category. The error can also distort carryover schedules and later-year planning.

## A practical classification worksheet

For every income item, record:

1. Payer and legal source.
2. Income character under U.S. law.
3. Country or countries involved.
4. Related deduction and expense allocation.
5. Foreign tax paid or accrued.
6. Proposed Form 1116 category and authority.

If one income item could fit more than one category, document the reason for the conclusion and flag it for review. The worksheet should reconcile to the Form 1040 and to the foreign tax evidence.

## Treaties and resourced income

A treaty can change the source of an item for limited purposes. That does not mean every treaty claim becomes general category income or that Form 8833 is always required. Review the treaty article, the saving-clause rules, Form 8833 instructions, and the Form 1116 instructions together.

Start with a complete fact inventory: filing year, U.S. status, residence and work locations, legal owner, dates, currencies, related entities, and prior filings. Gather primary documents before relying on summaries. Use the current agency instructions for the form or rule, preserve the source URL and access date, and write down any assumption that could change the result.

Then trace each fact to the return, information form, deadline, or disclosure it may affect. Reconcile the same item across the federal return, foreign return, account statements, and prior-year workpapers. The strongest way to use this page is to turn the question into a dated evidence packet rather than relying on a product label, a remembered balance, or a single tax-software screen. Start with the document that proves the legal relationship, then preserve the records that show what happened during the year. Keep the original file, the translated or summarized version if needed, and a short note explaining why the document belongs in the analysis.

It also prevents the same account or income item from being counted twice or omitted from a related form.

## Common failure modes

The most common failure is treating a general answer as a conclusion for a specific structure. A foreign label, a tax-free local result, a treaty headline, or an old filing can be a useful lead, but none is a substitute for the current form instructions and the governing documents. Another failure is collecting only year-end numbers. Many cross-border tests depend on a maximum value, a transaction during the year, a residence date, or an election that must be preserved in the return workpapers.

Do not delete ambiguous records. Keep them in a review queue with the question they create. A good queue distinguishes “document missing,” “classification uncertain,” “value needs reconstruction,” and “professional interpretation required.” That separation lets straightforward work continue while protecting the issues that should not be guessed.

Reconcile the draft against the source documents, prior-year filings, related information returns, and the final conversion or calculation worksheet. Confirm that names, identification numbers, dates, ownership percentages, currencies, and addresses are consistent.
