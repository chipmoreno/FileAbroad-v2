# Green Card Holders Abroad: Special Rules

Lawful permanent residence can continue to matter for U.S. tax even when the cardholder lives abroad. Start with the status and timeline, then review worldwide income, foreign accounts, foreign tax, and any treaty or expatriation position.

## Build the filing inventory

List wages, business income, investments, pensions, rentals, gifts, trusts, foreign companies, and accounts. Identify the forms that may connect to each item: Form 1040, FBAR, 8938, 3520, 5471, 8621, 8854, and Schedule E or C.

Keep immigration documents, foreign returns, account statements, and prior U.S. returns together. A missing information return can affect the analysis even when no U.S. income tax was due.

## Turn the topic into a repeatable review workflow

Start with a complete fact inventory: filing year, U.S. status, residence and work locations, legal owner, dates, currencies, related entities, and prior filings. Gather primary documents before relying on summaries. Use the current agency instructions for the form or rule, preserve the source URL and access date, and write down any assumption that could change the result.

Then trace each fact to the return, information form, deadline, or disclosure it may affect. Reconcile the same item across the federal return, foreign return, account statements, and prior-year workpapers. If the issue involves a local-law classification, treaty position, immigration status, willfulness, or a penalty, record it as an escalation question rather than presenting a general article as a conclusion.

## A practical evidence packet

The strongest way to use this page is to turn the question into a dated evidence packet rather than relying on a product label, a remembered balance, or a single tax-software screen. Start with the document that proves the legal relationship, then preserve the records that show what happened during the year. Keep the original file, the translated or summarized version if needed, and a short note explaining why the document belongs in the analysis.

Before deciding that a rule applies, record the filing year, your U.S. status, where you lived and worked, the legal owner of the asset or income, and any country-specific tax already paid. Those facts often change the result more than the name of the account or visa. Compare the result against [the green card holders abroad guide](/guides/green-card-holders-abroad), then use [the expat tax filing service](/services/expat-tax-filing) when the inventory needs a documented review.

Use a simple decision log with four columns: fact, source document, rule or form affected, and unresolved question. Mark assumptions separately from verified facts. This makes it easier to update the work when an institution corrects a statement, a treaty protocol changes, or the IRS and FinCEN publish new instructions. It also prevents the same account or income item from being counted twice or omitted from a related form.

## Common failure modes

The most common failure is treating a general answer as a conclusion for a specific structure. A foreign label, a tax-free local result, a treaty headline, or an old filing can be a useful lead, but none is a substitute for the current form instructions and the governing documents. Another failure is collecting only year-end numbers. Many cross-border tests depend on a maximum value, a transaction during the year, a residence date, or an election that must be preserved in the return workpapers.

Do not delete ambiguous records. Keep them in a review queue with the question they create. A good queue distinguishes “document missing,” “classification uncertain,” “value needs reconstruction,” and “professional interpretation required.” That separation lets straightforward work continue while protecting the issues that should not be guessed.

## Before submitting anything

Reconcile the draft against the source documents, prior-year filings, related information returns, and the final conversion or calculation worksheet. Confirm that names, identification numbers, dates, ownership percentages, currencies, and addresses are consistent. Save the filed copy, acceptance or confirmation record, and the working papers together. If the result depends on a legal opinion, treaty position, willfulness determination, immigration status, or a specialist classification, stop at the boundary described above and escalate that question rather than presenting an educational checklist as advice.

## Tax status is a timeline, not an address

Living outside the United States does not by itself end the tax analysis for a lawful permanent resident. Begin with the immigration status timeline and keep it separate from the tax-residence timeline. Record the date the green card was obtained, the years it was held, travel and residence dates, any abandonment or surrender event, and the date supporting documents were issued. A foreign residence permit or local tax residence certificate may be relevant, but it does not by itself answer every U.S. filing question.

For each filing year, identify whether the taxpayer filed a U.S. return, filed a foreign return, received an IRS notice, claimed a treaty position, or changed the way the green card status was treated. Preserve the applicable year’s IRS instructions and any immigration document that supports the date. When a treaty tie-breaker, residency position, abandonment question, or long-term-resident rule is involved, describe the open question and obtain qualified advice before signing a return or certification.

## Build a worldwide-income map

Create one row for every income source and one owner column for every asset. Include salary, contract work, business receipts, rental property, dividends, interest, capital gains, pensions, stock compensation, employer plans, royalties, digital assets, and distributions from foreign entities or trusts. Record the payer, legal owner, service location, payment date, original currency, withholding, and source document. Do not treat a local tax-free result as proof that the item is outside the U.S. reporting map.

Then map each row to the possible U.S. return schedule or information form. A wage item, foreign rental, foreign company, foreign fund, pension, trust distribution, gift, or account may require different questions even when the money arrived in one bank account. Keep income reporting, ownership reporting, FinCEN reporting, state filing, and treaty analysis as separate columns so one conclusion is not silently substituted for another.

## FEIE and foreign tax credit review

The foreign earned income exclusion and foreign tax credit are planning tools with different eligibility, income, timing, and documentation questions. Start by identifying the tax home, residence and travel pattern, where services were performed, the type of income, and foreign tax actually imposed. A taxpayer should not assume that living abroad creates an automatic exclusion, or that a foreign tax payment can be used in any way without a separate limitation and source analysis.

Keep a year-by-year schedule that shows earned income, days and locations, foreign tax paid or accrued, withholding evidence, exchange-rate method, prior elections, and the return position taken. Separate wages and self-employment from investment, pension, rental, or entity income. If the same income is being evaluated for an exclusion and a credit, document the interaction and confirm the current instructions before filing.

## Foreign accounts, funds, companies, and trusts

Account reporting begins with ownership and maximum value, not only the December balance. List bank, brokerage, pension, insurance, digital-asset, and other financial accounts, including accounts for which the taxpayer had signature or other authority. Compare the account inventory to the applicable FBAR and Form 8938 questions, recognizing that those forms have different tests and are not interchangeable.

For foreign companies, funds, partnerships, trusts, and gifts, preserve the governing documents and ownership history. A foreign corporation, investment fund, trust interest, or transfer may create a separate information-return question. Keep the entity or trust name, registration country, tax classification clue, ownership percentage, distributions, loans, and related-party transactions in the workpaper. Do not infer the U.S. classification from a local nickname such as “pension,” “mutual fund,” “foundation,” or “savings plan.”

## State domicile and the move abroad

Federal filing and state domicile are different workstreams. A former state may look at the date of departure, continuing contacts, housing, family, business activity, voting, driver’s license, professional registration, mail, and the taxpayer’s documented intent. A foreign address alone may not resolve the state question, and the state’s rules may differ from the federal rules or from the foreign country’s rules.

Create a departure packet with lease or sale documents, travel records, employment or business records, family location, account changes, vehicle and license records, voter registration, and written communications showing the move. Label evidence by date and avoid using a later action to rewrite an earlier year. If more than one state could claim a filing connection, list each state, the factual contact, the authority to review, and the unresolved issue.

## Abandonment and expatriation questions

The immigration event that ends or changes permanent-resident status and the federal tax analysis that may follow are related but not identical. Record the formal abandonment, surrender, or other status event, the date it became effective, the taxpayer’s prior years of residence, asset values, deferred compensation, pensions, trusts, and prior filing history. Preserve the relevant immigration record and the filing-year instructions.

Do not decide whether an exit-tax or long-term-resident rule applies from a headline or a single year’s income. The analysis can require historical tax liability, asset valuation, prior filings, certification history, and the exact status date. Form 8854 or another filing may be part of the review, but the correct conclusion depends on the facts and the applicable year. Immigration counsel, a tax professional, or another specialist may be needed for the part of the question outside a tax-preparation scope.

## If prior returns are missing or incomplete

Make a year matrix before choosing a catch-up path. For every year, record whether a Form 1040 was filed, whether foreign accounts or entities existed, whether notices were received, whether tax was paid locally, and which source documents are available. Separate missing returns from missing information forms, and separate an innocent records gap from a question about knowledge, intent, or a prior certification.

Preserve every notice and envelope, transcript, filed copy, acceptance record, foreign return, account statement, and prior workpaper. Do not backfill a number without describing how it was reconstructed. A written scope can define the years, deliverables, assumptions, records required, exclusions, and escalation points before any corrective filing is prepared.

## A green-card-holder document packet

The starting packet should include the green card and status records, passport and travel history, foreign residence evidence, U.S. and foreign addresses, employment and business records, foreign tax returns, wage and withholding statements, bank and brokerage statements, pension and insurance statements, entity and trust documents, property records, prior U.S. returns, IRS and state notices, and a list of every open question.

For each document, note the tax year, original language, original currency, owner, period covered, and the form or issue it may support. Keep translations or summaries with the original and identify who prepared the translation. Save the final filed copy and acceptance record alongside the workpapers so a future reviewer can trace the conclusion back to evidence.

## A year-by-year green card review worksheet

Use one worksheet row for each filing year. The row should show immigration status, tax-home location, residence dates, work locations, state connection, filing status, return filed, foreign return filed, foreign tax paid, accounts held, entities or trusts involved, notices received, and the source documents available. Add a final column for the question that remains unanswered. This makes a move, abandonment event, missing return, or change in ownership visible across years instead of treating the current year as a complete history.

For each year, compare the worksheet to the filed Form 1040, schedules, information returns, FBAR filing, foreign return, and bank or investment statements. A reconciliation should explain not only a positive amount but also a zero, blank, or missing form. If a prior preparer used a different classification, preserve the earlier filing and write down why the current review agrees or disagrees. Do not overwrite the history without a traceable explanation.

## Questions to ask before a move or status change

Before leaving the United States or changing permanent-resident status, identify the tax years that may be affected and the records that must be collected before accounts close or documents become difficult to obtain. Save final pay statements, business ledgers, property records, investment statements, pension and deferred-compensation documents, foreign tax evidence, travel history, and immigration records. A later address or a later status document may not prove what happened during an earlier filing period.

List every professional boundary in advance. A return preparer may be able to organize federal filing records, while immigration counsel, a state-tax adviser, valuation professional, or attorney may need to answer a status, domicile, valuation, treaty, or representation question. A written scope should say which questions are included, which are excluded, what the client must provide, and what event will trigger a referral.

## The final handoff

The completed file should contain a fact timeline, year matrix, income and account inventory, form map, source register, currency schedule, prior returns, notices, filed copies, acceptance records, and open-question log. Name files consistently by year and source so another reviewer can find the document behind each material conclusion. Record the date the official instructions and agency guidance were checked, since a future filing may use a different version.

If a conclusion depends on a document that has not arrived, mark it provisional and schedule a follow-up. If the issue has been referred, record the referred question and the professional responsible for answering it. A clean handoff protects the taxpayer from repeating the same history and gives the next reviewer a clear boundary between verified facts, assumptions, and advice.

## Review boundaries and next steps

Use the same worksheet after the return is filed. Add the submission date, acceptance record, information-return confirmations, and any follow-up notices. A post-filing check can catch an account, entity, or status document that was not included in the original inventory while the source records are still available.

Keep the worksheet with the next year’s source packet and refresh it when status, residence, ownership, or family facts change.

That continuing record is particularly useful when a future return depends on an earlier departure date, ownership transfer, election, or agency notice.
