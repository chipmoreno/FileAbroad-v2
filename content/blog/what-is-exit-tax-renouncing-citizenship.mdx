# What Is the Exit Tax When Renouncing U.S. Citizenship?

The U.S. exit tax is not a simple fee charged when someone signs a renunciation certificate. It is an expatriation-tax regime that can apply to a former citizen or long-term lawful permanent resident who is a covered expatriate. The analysis combines status, dates, tax compliance, net worth, prior tax liability, assets, deferred compensation, and Form 8854.

## The covered-expatriate review

The current Form 8854 instructions and Internal Revenue Code rules identify the tests. A review commonly considers:

- Whether the person expatriated on the relevant date.
- Average U.S. income-tax liability for the specified prior years.
- Net worth on the expatriation date.
- Whether the person can certify five years of U.S. federal tax compliance.
- Deferred compensation, trusts, gifts, and other special assets.

Thresholds and filing-year amounts can change. Do not use a number from an old article as a personal conclusion.

## Deemed-sale concept

For a covered expatriate, the mark-to-market regime can treat property as sold for fair market value on the day before expatriation, subject to the applicable exclusion and rules. The result depends on basis, value, property type, holding structure, and the current statute. Deferred compensation and specified tax-deferred accounts can follow different rules.

## Compliance comes before the date

A person considering renunciation should reconstruct the prior compliance record early. Missing Forms 8938, 3520, 5471, 8621, FBARs, or returns can affect the certification and the risk assessment. Filing a return does not automatically prove that every international information return was complete.

## Immigration and tax work are different

The State Department or an embassy handles the nationality process. A tax preparer cannot give immigration advice, and an immigration action does not calculate the tax. Coordinate the dates and documents with qualified professionals.

## Common failure modes

The most common failure is treating a general answer as a conclusion for a specific structure. A foreign label, a tax-free local result, a treaty headline, or an old filing can be a useful lead, but none is a substitute for the current form instructions and the governing documents. Another failure is collecting only year-end numbers. Many cross-border tests depend on a maximum value, a transaction during the year, a residence date, or an election that must be preserved in the return workpapers.

Do not delete ambiguous records. Keep them in a review queue with the question they create. A good queue distinguishes “document missing,” “classification uncertain,” “value needs reconstruction,” and “professional interpretation required.” That separation lets straightforward work continue while protecting the issues that should not be guessed.
