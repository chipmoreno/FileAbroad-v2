# Roth Conversions While Living Abroad: What Expats Should Model

A Roth conversion can be useful for long-term retirement planning, but moving abroad adds layers that are easy to miss. The U.S. conversion calculation, foreign-country treatment, treaty rules, state domicile, and future distributions should be modeled together.

## The U.S. conversion is generally an income event

When previously untaxed traditional IRA amounts are converted to a Roth IRA, the converted amount can be included in U.S. gross income. The taxable amount depends on basis and the account facts. A taxpayer should not assume that a conversion is excluded because the income is not wages or because the taxpayer qualifies for the FEIE.

The conversion can affect:

- The marginal rate on other income.
- The foreign tax credit limitation.
- The FEIE stacking calculation.
- Premiums, credits, and other income-tested items.
- State tax residency and domicile.
- Future required minimum distributions and beneficiary planning.

## FEIE and FTC interaction

The FEIE generally excludes qualifying foreign earned income, not retirement conversions. The excluded income can still affect the rate applied to non-excluded income. A foreign tax credit may be available for eligible foreign income tax, but the limitation is based on foreign-source income and separate categories. A conversion should be modeled in the actual return, not added to a simple “tax-free abroad” assumption.

## Foreign-country and treaty review

The country of residence may tax the conversion, defer it, treat it as a pension distribution, or apply a different rule. Treaty provisions can modify the result, but treaty claims have their own requirements and do not replace local advice. The timing of the conversion, account contributions, previous residence, and account type can all matter.

## Questions to answer before converting

1. What is the traditional IRA’s after-tax basis?
2. What other income will be reported in the conversion year?
3. Is the taxpayer using the FEIE or FTC, and what is the long-term plan?
4. Which country taxes the taxpayer and what treaty provisions could apply?
5. Does the taxpayer still have state domicile or filing exposure?
6. Will the account owner need a withholding or estimated-tax plan?
7. Are there inherited-account or beneficiary rules that change the timing?

## Turn the topic into a repeatable review workflow

Start with a complete fact inventory: filing year, U.S. status, residence and work locations, legal owner, dates, currencies, related entities, and prior filings. Gather primary documents before relying on summaries. Use the current agency instructions for the form or rule, preserve the source URL and access date, and write down any assumption that could change the result.

Then trace each fact to the return, information form, deadline, or disclosure it may affect. Reconcile the same item across the federal return, foreign return, account statements, and prior-year workpapers. If the issue involves a local-law classification, treaty position, immigration status, willfulness, or a penalty, record it as an escalation question rather than presenting a general article as a conclusion.

Start with the document that proves the legal relationship, then preserve the records that show what happened during the year. Keep the original file, the translated or summarized version if needed, and a short note explaining why the document belongs in the analysis.

Before deciding that a rule applies, record the filing year, your U.S. status, where you lived and worked, the legal owner of the asset or income, and any country-specific tax already paid. ## Common failure modes

The most common failure is treating a general answer as a conclusion for a specific structure. A foreign label, a tax-free local result, a treaty headline, or an old filing can be a useful lead, but none is a substitute for the current form instructions and the governing documents. Another failure is collecting only year-end numbers. Many cross-border tests depend on a maximum value, a transaction during the year, a residence date, or an election that must be preserved in the return workpapers.

Do not delete ambiguous records. Keep them in a review queue with the question they create. A good queue distinguishes “document missing,” “classification uncertain,” “value needs reconstruction,” and “professional interpretation required.” That separation lets straightforward work continue while protecting the issues that should not be guessed.

## Before submitting anything

Reconcile the draft against the source documents, prior-year filings, related information returns, and the final conversion or calculation worksheet. Confirm that names, identification numbers, dates, ownership percentages, currencies, and addresses are consistent. Save the filed copy, acceptance or confirmation record, and the working papers together. If the result depends on a legal opinion, treaty position, willfulness determination, immigration status, or a specialist classification, stop at the boundary described above and escalate that question rather than presenting an educational checklist as advice.

## Model the conversion year before choosing a date

Build a projected return using the actual tax year, filing status, other income, withholding, foreign tax, deductions, credits, and retirement balances. Run at least two cases: no conversion and the proposed conversion. If the conversion is split across months, model the amount and withholding for each transaction. Include state tax possibilities and the country where the taxpayer is resident. A conversion that looks attractive under a federal-only estimate can create a foreign tax, state, withholding, or future-year issue.

The model should show ordinary income, capital gains, FEIE-eligible earned income, foreign-source income, foreign taxes, and retirement distributions separately. Do not put every item into one effective-rate calculation. The Foreign Tax Credit limitation may be affected by income category and source, and the FEIE stacking rule can change the rate applied to income that remains taxable. Preserve the assumptions and source documents used for each scenario.

## Basis and account history matter

Request the traditional IRA statement, contribution history, prior Forms 8606, rollover records, inherited-account documents, conversion confirmations, and year-end value. If the taxpayer has more than one traditional, SEP, or SIMPLE IRA, review the pro-rata calculation rather than treating the conversion account in isolation. If the account was inherited, identify the owner, beneficiary, distribution rules, and date of death.

A foreign pension or local retirement account is not automatically a U.S. IRA. Do not import the U.S. Roth-conversion rules into a foreign plan without reviewing the plan document and any treaty. The account may have a separate U.S. classification, information-reporting requirement, or local tax result. If the proposed conversion is designed to change future RMDs or beneficiary outcomes, keep the investment and estate-planning decision separate from the tax preparation scope.

## Withholding and payment planning from abroad

A taxpayer living outside the United States may have difficulty changing U.S. withholding, paying an estimated tax installment, or coordinating local withholding. Record the payment method, currency, settlement date, and whether the amount was withheld or paid separately. A withholding choice is not the same as the final liability. The return must reconcile the statement, the conversion amount, basis, and credits.

Do not use the conversion to create a false impression of earned income. Roth-conversion income is not wages and does not become FEIE-eligible merely because the taxpayer lives abroad. It can, however, affect the rate applied to other income, eligibility for some deductions or credits, and the foreign-country computation. Confirm the classification on both sides of the border.

## Foreign country and treaty questions

The residence country may tax the conversion when made, when distributed, or under a pension provision. Some countries treat a U.S. Roth account differently from a traditional IRA; others may not recognize the Roth treatment at all. Obtain the local assessment, pension or account statement, and treaty text or professional interpretation used. A treaty may allocate taxing rights without making a local return unnecessary.

If the country taxes the conversion, determine whether the tax is creditable in the United States and in which category. Keep evidence of payment, not just a local calculator. If the country does not tax it, do not assume a U.S. foreign tax credit is available from other income without the required limitation work. The conversion year should contain a short memo explaining the cross-border result.

## State and domicile review

Former residents of persistent-tax states should review domicile before setting a conversion date. A state may tax a resident’s worldwide income, have different conformity rules, or treat retirement income and federal exclusions differently. A foreign address does not automatically end state residence. Preserve the move timeline, home availability, family location, visits, voter and license records, and state-source income schedule.

If the taxpayer remains a state resident, model the federal and state results together. If the taxpayer became a nonresident, identify whether the conversion has state-source treatment under the current state instructions. Keep the state conclusion in a separate workpaper so a federal treaty or FEIE assumption is not mistaken for a state rule.

## A decision checklist for the appointment

Bring the proposed conversion amount and date, account statements, Forms 8606, prior returns, foreign residence and tax records, state-departure evidence, other income estimates, withholding details, beneficiary information, and the tax professional or investment adviser’s model. Identify what decision is actually being made: whether to convert, how much, when, how to pay, or how to report an already completed conversion.

## A conversion workpaper should survive a future review

Save the account statement before and after the conversion, the request or trade confirmation, Form 8606 history, withholding evidence, bank payment records, foreign return treatment, treaty source, and state-residency workpaper. Add a one-page summary that explains the amount, date, basis, other income, tax payment plan, and unresolved questions. If the taxpayer changes residence later, preserve the original country analysis and record what changed.

The workpaper should distinguish a completed transaction from a proposed transaction. A completed conversion belongs in the return and should be reconciled to the statement. A proposed conversion is a planning scenario and should not be represented as a filing fact. If a financial adviser recommended the amount, keep the investment recommendation separate from the tax calculation and identify who is responsible for each conclusion.

Refresh the model when tax law, treaty text, residence, income, or account basis changes.
