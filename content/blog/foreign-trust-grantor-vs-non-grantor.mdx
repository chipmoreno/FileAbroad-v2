# Foreign Grantor Trust vs. Nongrantor Trust for U.S. Tax Reporting

The words “grantor” and “nongrantor” describe a U.S. tax analysis, not necessarily the name used in the country where a trust was created. A foreign family trust, foundation, private settlement, or similar arrangement must be reviewed under its instrument and the U.S. ownership rules before deciding which forms apply.

## The central distinction

A **foreign grantor trust** is generally treated as owned by a person under the grantor-trust rules. The U.S. owner may be required to take the trust’s income into account and may have information-reporting obligations related to the trust. A **foreign nongrantor trust** is treated as a separate taxable entity to the extent the assets are not treated as owned by another person.

The distinction can be partial. A trust can be treated as grantor-owned for one portion or owner and nongrantor for another portion. The answer does not come from a bank statement or a marketing description.

## Why Form 3520-A matters

The IRS Form 3520-A instructions address the annual information return for a foreign trust with a U.S. owner. The form can provide a foreign grantor trust owner statement or beneficiary statement, and the owner may need to attach a substitute Form 3520-A to Form 3520 when the foreign trust does not file as required.

The trust’s documents and statements should be reviewed for:

- The trust tax year and accounting period.
- The identity and status of the owner, grantor, trustee, and beneficiaries.
- Trust income, expenses, distributions, and loans.
- The U.S. agent and whether the trust can produce records or testimony.
- The ownership explanation and beneficiary statement required by the instructions.

## How distributions are analyzed

A distribution from a foreign grantor trust can be treated differently from a distribution from a foreign nongrantor trust. The grantor-trust beneficiary statement can allow a beneficiary to apply the treatment of the owner in the circumstances described by the instructions. A nongrantor-trust distribution may require a distribution statement, accumulated-distribution analysis, and additional records to determine the U.S. tax result.

Do not call a transfer a “gift” until the trust classification and transfer path have been reviewed. The same payment can raise different reporting questions depending on whether it came from the trust, the grantor, another beneficiary, or a foreign person acting through an intermediary.

## Common mistakes

1. Treating a foreign trust as a domestic trust because the trustee speaks English.
2. Treating a foundation or insurance wrapper as outside the trust rules without reviewing the legal documents.
3. Reporting the distribution but not the ownership or transfer event.
4. Using the prior year’s statement when the trust instrument or beneficiaries changed.
5. Assuming a Form 8938 entry replaces Form 3520 or Form 3520-A.

## Common failure modes

The most common failure is treating a general answer as a conclusion for a specific structure. A foreign label, a tax-free local result, a treaty headline, or an old filing can be a useful lead, but none is a substitute for the current form instructions and the governing documents. Another failure is collecting only year-end numbers. Many cross-border tests depend on a maximum value, a transaction during the year, a residence date, or an election that must be preserved in the return workpapers.

Do not delete ambiguous records. Keep them in a review queue with the question they create. A good queue distinguishes “document missing,” “classification uncertain,” “value needs reconstruction,” and “professional interpretation required.” That separation lets straightforward work continue while protecting the issues that should not be guessed.
