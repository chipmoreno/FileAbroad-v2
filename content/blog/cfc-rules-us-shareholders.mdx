# CFC Rules for U.S. Shareholders Living Abroad

Running a company abroad does not end a U.S. person’s reporting obligations. A foreign corporation can create annual Form 5471 work and potential income inclusions even when the company does not distribute cash to its U.S. shareholder.

## Start with the entity and ownership chain

The analysis begins with the foreign company’s legal form, tax classification, shareholders, voting rights, value, and ownership changes. Attribution and related-party rules can matter, so a shareholder cannot always rely only on the number of shares registered in their name.

The review should identify:

- The corporation’s jurisdiction, accounting period, and functional currency.
- Direct, indirect, and constructive ownership.
- U.S. shareholders and changes during the year.
- Related foreign entities, partnerships, branches, and disregarded entities.
- Distributions, loans, guarantees, related-party transactions, and reorganizations.

## Why Form 5471 is not one simple return

The IRS describes several Form 5471 filing categories for U.S. persons related to foreign corporations. The correct category affects which schedules are required. A complete and separate form may be required for each foreign corporation, and the schedules can require book-to-tax adjustments, earnings and profits, balance sheets, income statements, ownership changes, related-party transactions, and other details.

A company can have a filing obligation even if no U.S. income inclusion is ultimately calculated. Conversely, an income inclusion can exist even when the corporation never sends a dividend.

## Subpart F and GILTI are different questions

Subpart F generally targets specified categories of income and transactions under the international tax rules. GILTI is a separate regime involving controlled foreign corporations, tested income, tested loss, qualified business asset investment, foreign taxes, and other rules. The calculation can be affected by the corporation’s business activity, related-party transactions, depreciation, foreign tax, and shareholder status.

Do not describe a business as “active, so there is no U.S. tax.” Active business income can still be relevant to Form 5471 and the applicable inclusion regimes.

## Documents to collect

Before a CFC review, collect the articles and amendments, shareholder register, capitalization table, financial statements, trial balance, general ledger, foreign tax returns, distributions, intercompany agreements, payroll records, loans, and prior Form 5471 filings. The tax year of the corporation may not match the U.S. tax year, and currency translation must be supported.

## Common failure points

- Treating a foreign LLC’s local classification as its U.S. classification.
- Ignoring constructive ownership through a spouse, family member, trust, or related entity.
- Filing only the first Form 5471 schedule and omitting required schedules.
- Reusing the prior-year file after ownership or accounting changes.
- Assuming a payment is a dividend without analyzing the entity and earnings history.

## Turn the topic into a repeatable review workflow

Start with a complete fact inventory: filing year, U.S. status, residence and work locations, legal owner, dates, currencies, related entities, and prior filings. Gather primary documents before relying on summaries. Use the current agency instructions for the form or rule, preserve the source URL and access date, and write down any assumption that could change the result.

Then trace each fact to the return, information form, deadline, or disclosure it may affect. Reconcile the same item across the federal return, foreign return, account statements, and prior-year workpapers. If the issue involves a local-law classification, treaty position, immigration status, willfulness, or a penalty, record it as an escalation question rather than presenting a general article as a conclusion.

## Common failure modes

The most common failure is treating a general answer as a conclusion for a specific structure. A foreign label, a tax-free local result, a treaty headline, or an old filing can be a useful lead, but none is a substitute for the current form instructions and the governing documents. Another failure is collecting only year-end numbers. Many cross-border tests depend on a maximum value, a transaction during the year, a residence date, or an election that must be preserved in the return workpapers.

Do not delete ambiguous records. Keep them in a review queue with the question they create. A good queue distinguishes “document missing,” “classification uncertain,” “value needs reconstruction,” and “professional interpretation required.” That separation lets straightforward work continue while protecting the issues that should not be guessed.
