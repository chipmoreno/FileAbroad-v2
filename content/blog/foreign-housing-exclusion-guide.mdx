The **Foreign Housing Exclusion** (FHE) is a Form 2555 calculation for eligible taxpayers who meet the tax-home and FEIE qualification rules. It is not a general rent deduction: the employee exclusion is tied to qualifying employer-provided amounts, while self-employed taxpayers use the separate housing-deduction rules.

## What Is the Foreign Housing Exclusion?

The FHE is a companion benefit to the FEIE. It allows you to exclude qualifying foreign housing expenses from your gross income, but only if:

1. You **qualify for the FEIE** (Physical Presence Test or Bona Fide Residence Test).
2. Your housing expenses are **paid with employer-provided amounts** (for employees).
3. Your expenses exceed a **base housing amount**.

## How the FHE Calculation Works

### Step 1: Determine Your Qualifying Housing Expenses

Add up expenses paid during your qualifying period:
- Rent
- Utilities (except telephone)
- Property insurance
- Repairs and maintenance
- Residential parking
- Nonrefundable fees paid to obtain a lease

### Step 2: Apply the Annual Location Limit

The IRS publishes annual location limits. For 2025, the general limit for a full qualifying year when the location was not listed in the annual notice was **$39,000**; listed locations used the applicable amount in Notice 2025-16. A partial qualifying period uses the applicable daily amount. Limits change by tax year, so do not carry a city figure from one year into another.

### Step 3: Subtract the Base Housing Amount

The base amount is generally **16% of the applicable FEIE maximum**:
- For 2025: **$20,800** ($130,000 × 16%)

**Exclusion amount = Lesser of (actual expenses or location limit) – base amount**

### Example

The calculation is not simply rent minus the base amount. It also depends on whether the amount was employer-provided, the annual location limit, the qualifying days, and the taxpayer's foreign earned income. A Form 2555 calculation can therefore produce a different result from a rent-only estimate.

## Qualifying vs. Non-Qualifying Expenses

| Qualifying | Non-Qualifying |
|---|---|
| Rent | Deductible interest and taxes; mortgage principal |
| Utilities (except phone) | Furniture and furnishings |
| Property insurance | Domestic help (maids, cooks) |
| Repairs and maintenance | Buying or improving a home |
| Nonrefundable lease fees | Pay television |
| Residential parking | Amounts reimbursed by employer |

## Foreign Housing Exclusion vs. Foreign Housing Deduction

| | Exclusion (Employees) | Deduction (Self-Employed) |
|---|---|---|
| **Who qualifies** | Employees with employer-provided housing | Self-employed individuals |
| **How it works** | Excludes housing amounts from gross income | Deducts housing expenses from income |
| **Maximum benefit** | More generous | Less generous |
| **Self-employment tax** | N/A | Does not reduce SE tax |
| **Form** | Form 2555, Parts VI and VIII | Form 2555, Parts VI and IX |

**Self-employed individuals:** You generally use the Foreign Housing **Deduction**, not the employee exclusion. Form 2555 has separate rules for the deduction, including a possible one-year carryover; calculate it from the applicable instructions rather than assuming it matches the employee exclusion.

## When to Use FHE vs. FTC

The FHE and the Foreign Tax Credit (FTC) are not mutually exclusive, but choosing the right combination matters.

### Use FHE When:
- You live in a **low-tax or no-tax country**.
- Your **housing costs are high** relative to your income.
- You are an **employee** with employer-provided housing benefits.
- Your **foreign tax liability is low**, so the FTC provides minimal benefit.

### Use FTC Instead When:
- You live in a **high-tax country** (Germany, France, UK, Japan).
- Your **foreign taxes already offset** most or all of your US liability.
- Your **housing costs are modest** and do not justify the FHE.
- The **stacking effect** of FEIE + FHE pushes your remaining income into high brackets.

### Possible Combination

Claim **both**:
1. Calculate the **FEIE** and housing benefit, if eligible.
2. Separately calculate any **FTC** available for foreign taxes allocable to income that was not excluded.

This requires careful calculation; the combination is not automatically the lowest-tax result.

## Common Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Claiming Non-Qualifying Expenses

The Form 2555 instructions exclude items such as furniture, deductible interest and taxes, domestic labor, and reimbursed amounts. Keep detailed records and claim only expenses that fit the applicable instructions.

### Mistake 2: Missing the Employer-Funded Requirement

For employees, the FHE only applies to housing amounts **provided by your employer**. If you pay rent out of your own post-tax salary, you may not qualify for the exclusion (though you may qualify for the deduction if self-employed).

### Mistake 3: Using the Wrong City Limit

The IRS publishes specific limits for hundreds of cities. Using a generic limit instead of the city-specific limit can result in either underclaiming or overclaiming.

### Mistake 4: Not Prorating for Partial Years

If you qualify for only part of the year, you must prorate the base amount and the housing limit.

## How to Document FHE Claims

Maintain records of:
- Lease agreements or rental contracts.
- Monthly rent receipts or bank transfers.
- Utility bills.
- Property insurance invoices.
- Repair and maintenance receipts.
- Employer statements showing housing allowance (if applicable).
