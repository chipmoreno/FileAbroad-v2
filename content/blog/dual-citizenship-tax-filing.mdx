Being a citizen of the United States and another country can make a tax file feel like two systems are competing for the same income. The practical answer is more structured: classify the person, identify each country’s residence rules, map the income and assets, then apply the current U.S. forms and treaty provisions to the same records.

U.S. citizenship generally continues to matter even when a dual citizen has never lived in the United States as an adult. The federal return, foreign-account reports, foreign asset forms, pension treatment, business reporting, and local filings each have their own rules. Start with the current [IRS Publication 54](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p54) and the [expat tax guide](/guides/expat-tax-guide), then build a year-specific filing map.

## Citizenship, residence, and domicile are different questions

Citizenship describes legal status. Tax residence is determined under the laws of each country and may depend on home, days, family, work, economic ties, or a treaty tie-breaker. Domicile can matter for a U.S. state even after federal residence changes. Do not use a passport, a local tax number, or a residence card as a shortcut for all three questions.

For each tax year, write a timeline. List where you slept, where you worked, where your spouse or children lived, which homes were available, where your business was managed, and where you were registered for local tax. Record temporary travel separately from the move that established a long-term home. The purpose is not to produce a perfect narrative after the fact; it is to reconcile the return to contemporaneous evidence.

A dual citizen can be resident in the other country under local law and still have a U.S. filing obligation. The foreign return may be important evidence and may also produce foreign taxes that are relevant to the U.S. return. It does not automatically replace the Form 1040, FBAR, or other U.S. information returns.

## Build the worldwide income inventory

List earned income first: wages, self-employment, contract work, director fees, bonuses, equity compensation, and business distributions. Record the payer, work location, service dates, currency, gross amount, withholding, local form, and whether the income was received personally or through an entity. The place of the bank account receiving the money does not necessarily answer the source question.

Then list investment and retirement income: interest, dividends, gains, rental income, pensions, annuities, social security, trust distributions, and withdrawals from local savings plans. Capture the account or institution, owner, maximum value, year-end value, contributions, distributions, and local tax treatment. A tax-free result under local law can still be reportable or taxable under U.S. law.

For business owners, add entity formation documents, ownership percentages, financial statements, retained earnings, loans, compensation, and management location. A local company may trigger controlled foreign corporation, partnership, or foreign trust analysis. A dual citizen who inherited a company, opened a family trust, or bought local mutual funds should not assume those assets are ordinary U.S. brokerage holdings.

## Foreign tax: credit, exclusion, or treaty analysis

Foreign tax is not simply subtracted from U.S. tax. The Foreign Tax Credit has source, basket, limitation, timing, and documentation rules. Some taxes qualify; others do not. A refund or later adjustment in the foreign country can change the U.S. calculation. Preserve the foreign assessment, payment evidence, withholding statement, return, refund notice, and currency conversion method.

The Foreign Earned Income Exclusion may apply to qualifying earned income when the taxpayer meets the relevant tax-home and residence or presence test. It does not cover every type of income, and choosing it can affect other calculations. Review the [FEIE guide](/guides/feie-guide) and compare it with the [Foreign Tax Credit guide](/guides/foreign-tax-credit-guide) before assuming the exclusion is best.

Tax treaties can alter residence, pension, employment, scholarship, or other outcomes, but a treaty position usually requires a precise article, facts, and disclosure. A treaty is not a general exemption for dual citizens. Start with the [IRS treaty guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/tax-treaties-can-affect-your-income-tax), identify the article relied upon, and document the local and U.S. residence conclusions that make the article relevant.

## Foreign accounts and assets

The FBAR and Form 8938 are separate reports. The FBAR is filed with FinCEN and focuses on qualifying foreign financial accounts and the applicable aggregate maximum-balance test. Form 8938 is attached to the federal return and uses different asset definitions and thresholds. Some accounts appear on both; some appear on one; filing one does not replace the other.

Make an account inventory with the legal owner, joint owners, signature authority, account type, institution country, account number or safe internal identifier, maximum balance, currency, exchange rate, and closing date if the account was closed. Include local bank accounts, securities accounts, insurance products with cash value, pension accounts, and accounts held through a foreign entity when the current instructions require analysis.

Dual citizens often have accounts opened in childhood or inherited from family. Those accounts can be easy to omit because the taxpayer does not think of them as investment accounts. Ask about dormant accounts, local savings books, employer pension plans, mobile-wallet balances, and accounts where a parent remains the formal owner. Do not guess the classification from the institution’s marketing name; obtain the governing documents and statement description.

## Local pensions, funds, and savings products

Foreign pensions rarely fit neatly into a U.S. account category. The treaty, the plan terms, contributions, distributions, employer relationship, and local tax treatment can all matter. A local retirement account may be reportable, taxable on a different schedule, or subject to a treaty election. Keep annual statements, plan rules, beneficiary records, and distribution notices.

Foreign mutual funds and exchange-traded products can create PFIC analysis. A dual citizen may buy a local fund that feels identical to a U.S. mutual fund but is classified differently for U.S. purposes. Gather the fund prospectus, annual statements, NAV history, distributions, purchase lots, and ownership dates. The [PFIC guide](/guides/pfic-guide) and [Form 8621 page](/forms/8621-pfic) explain why an ordinary local investment can require a specialized review.

Canadian RRSPs, Australian superannuation, UK pensions, and employer plans from other countries each need their own treaty and reporting review. Do not apply a Canadian rule to a UK plan or a pension rule to an insurance product merely because both are called “retirement.”

## State tax can survive an international move

Federal citizenship rules and state residency rules are separate. A former New York, California, Virginia, or Massachusetts resident may need to analyze domicile, statutory residency, the availability of a home, family ties, work location, and state-source income even after establishing a foreign home. A foreign tax return does not prove that state domicile ended.

Create a state-departure file for the year of the move. Record the date the U.S. home was sold, rented, or retained; the foreign lease; days in the former state; driver license and voter status; family location; employer and business records; and any state return or notice. The [state taxes abroad guide](/guides/state-taxes-abroad) gives the framework, but the correct result is fact-specific.

## Common dual-citizen mistakes

One mistake is filing only in the country where the taxpayer works. Another is assuming a local tax number is a replacement for an SSN or ITIN. Others include reporting a bank account on an FBAR but omitting the interest on Form 1040, claiming a foreign tax credit without a tax statement, treating a foreign pension as a U.S. IRA, or forgetting a prior-year election.

A second category is inconsistent records. The foreign return may list a different income amount or residence date than the U.S. return. Currency conversion may be based on a different period. A business may pay salary in one country and dividends in another. Reconcile the differences in a workpaper; do not silently force the two returns to match.

The third category is overconfidence about treaties. An article title, a bank’s statement, or a tax preparer’s general comment is not the same as a treaty position supported by the current text, protocol, and facts. Preserve the reasoning and escalate specialist issues.

## A practical annual workflow

At the start of the process, identify citizenships, tax residences, states, filing status, prior filings, and missing years. Next, request income and account records for every country. Then classify each item before calculating the return. After that, prepare the federal return, information forms, foreign tax workpapers, treaty disclosures, state returns, and local return together enough to catch contradictions.

Before filing, compare names, addresses, ownership, dates, currencies, maximum values, and tax paid. Confirm that a form is attached to the return when required and filed separately when required. Save acceptance records, a PDF of the final filing, source documents, conversion rates, and a short issues log for the next year.

If the file includes unfiled years, foreign companies, PFICs, trusts, notices, a state domicile dispute, or a treaty position, start with a paid consultation. ## A dual-citizen records checklist

Ask for the same categories every year, even when the taxpayer says nothing changed. The core packet includes passports and citizenship records, foreign residence evidence, travel calendar, U.S. and foreign returns, wage and contractor statements, bank statements, brokerage statements, pension records, business accounts, local tax assessments, withholding certificates, and prior U.S. filings. Add a note for every item that was closed, sold, transferred, inherited, or converted.

For accounts, capture both the maximum value and the year-end value where relevant. For assets, capture cost basis, acquisition date, owner, currency, and whether the value is personal, joint, trust-owned, or entity-owned. For income, capture the accrual or payment date, source country, payer, local tax, and conversion method. This structure makes later form decisions auditable instead of relying on memory.

Keep foreign-language documents with a short English description. The description is not a replacement for a required translation, but it helps classify the record. Note whether a statement shows gross income, net income, tax withheld, tax assessed, or a balance. Local bank statements often show several of these concepts on separate lines.

## When a prior year was missed

A dual citizen who discovers missing U.S. returns should first stop and map the facts. List every year, income source, account, entity, pension, foreign tax, and U.S. notice. Determine which returns, FBARs, and information forms were required, whether tax was due, and whether the taxpayer was living abroad under the applicable procedure. Do not select a catch-up program because it sounds simplest.

The [catch-up program tool](/tools/catch-up-program) can organize the questions, but it is not an eligibility determination. Review the residence facts, filing history, payment history, and any contact from the IRS before relying on a streamlined or delinquent filing path. If the facts raise willfulness, prior professional advice, amended returns, or a notice response, obtain specialist review.

Prepare a chronology of what the taxpayer knew and when. Include prior preparer conversations, bank letters, local filing notices, account openings, citizenship discovery, and any attempts to obtain an SSN or ITIN. The chronology is not a confession or legal conclusion; it preserves facts that may be relevant to the appropriate process and response.

## Reconcile the two countries before filing

Make a cross-border comparison sheet with one row per item. Columns should include the local return label, U.S. return label, gross amount, local tax, U.S. tax treatment, source, currency, conversion rate, and supporting record. Mark items that appear only on the local return or only on the U.S. return and explain the difference. A mismatch is often legitimate, but an unexplained mismatch is a future notice risk.

Examples include employer pension contributions treated as deductible locally but not in the United States, tax-free savings products treated as taxable investment accounts, or a local capital gain calculated under a different basis and holding-period rule. Do not force the U.S. return to mirror the local tax computation. Instead, preserve the bridge between the two systems and identify the U.S. authority used.

After filing, save the final local return and U.S. return together with the bridge. This is especially important when the foreign tax credit is carried forward, a treaty election continues, or an account will be reported differently after a distribution. A clean bridge makes next year’s work faster and reduces the risk of repeating a classification error.
