# PFIC Mark-to-Market Election: When It May Apply

The mark-to-market election can be a practical alternative when a PFIC does not provide the information needed for a Qualified Electing Fund election. It is not available for every foreign fund, and it changes when gains and losses are recognized.

## Start with eligibility

The current Form 8621 instructions generally connect the election to marketable PFIC stock. Review whether the stock is regularly traded on a qualified exchange or market and whether the holding structure preserves that status. A fund being listed somewhere is not a complete eligibility conclusion.

Collect the fund name, legal domicile, ticker, exchange, pricing history, share count, acquisition dates, adjusted basis, and year-end fair market value. If the asset is held through a pension, trust, partnership, or entity, analyze the ownership chain before treating it as directly held stock.

## How the annual calculation works

Under the mark-to-market regime, annual appreciation can be included as ordinary income, subject to the statutory calculation. Losses are not a general capital-loss deduction; the rules limit the deductible amount and coordinate it with previously included mark-to-market gains. On a later sale, the adjusted basis and prior inclusions must be reconciled.

## Timing matters

The election is usually most useful when considered before or at the beginning of the holding period. A late election can cause the PFIC to be treated as sold at fair market value on the last day of the election year, with the gain treated under the excess-distribution rules. That deemed transaction can create tax and interest that a taxpayer did not expect.

Do not check the election box until the prior-year Form 8621 history, the basis, and the marketability evidence have been reviewed.

## Mark-to-market versus QEF

A QEF election generally requires an Annual Information Statement from the fund. Mark-to-market does not use that same statement, but it can produce ordinary-income treatment and requires reliable market values. The correct choice depends on fund information, timing, tax rates, liquidity, and the taxpayer’s objective.

## Assemble a holding-by-holding PFIC file

For a PFIC question, identify the issuer and the legal product first. Collect prospectuses, annual reports, NAV statements, purchase and sale confirmations, distributions, basis records, and any available Qualified Electing Fund information. Record the number of shares or units held, acquisition dates, holding periods, year-end value, and whether the item was held directly, through an account, or through another entity. A familiar local fund or savings product can still require a U.S. classification review.

Map each holding to the possible U.S. reporting method and election history. Check whether a QEF or mark-to-market election was made, when it became effective, and whether the supporting annual information is complete. If no election applies, preserve the distribution and disposition history needed for the default calculation. Do not estimate missing basis or annual information without labeling the assumption and escalating the result.

## A practical evidence packet

The strongest way to use this page is to turn the question into a dated evidence packet rather than relying on a product label, a remembered balance, or a single tax-software screen. Start with the document that proves the legal relationship, then preserve the records that show what happened during the year. Keep the original file, the translated or summarized version if needed, and a short note explaining why the document belongs in the analysis.

Before deciding that a rule applies, record the filing year, your U.S. status, where you lived and worked, the legal owner of the asset or income, and any country-specific tax already paid. Those facts often change the result more than the name of the account or visa. Compare the result against [the pfic guide](/guides/pfic-guide), then use [the expat tax filing service](/services/expat-tax-filing) when the inventory needs a documented review.

Use a simple decision log with four columns: fact, source document, rule or form affected, and unresolved question. Mark assumptions separately from verified facts. This makes it easier to update the work when an institution corrects a statement, a treaty protocol changes, or the IRS and FinCEN publish new instructions. It also prevents the same account or income item from being counted twice or omitted from a related form.

## Common failure modes

The most common failure is treating a general answer as a conclusion for a specific structure. A foreign label, a tax-free local result, a treaty headline, or an old filing can be a useful lead, but none is a substitute for the current form instructions and the governing documents. Another failure is collecting only year-end numbers. Many cross-border tests depend on a maximum value, a transaction during the year, a residence date, or an election that must be preserved in the return workpapers.

Do not delete ambiguous records. Keep them in a review queue with the question they create. A good queue distinguishes “document missing,” “classification uncertain,” “value needs reconstruction,” and “professional interpretation required.” That separation lets straightforward work continue while protecting the issues that should not be guessed.
