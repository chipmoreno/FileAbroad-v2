# Canadian RRSPs and TFSAs for US Taxpayers: Treaty Protection and Traps

Canada is one of the most common cross-border planning contexts for U.S. taxpayers. RRSP/RRIF treatment has specific treaty and administrative authority, while a TFSA requires a separate account-and-holdings analysis.

## Canadian RRSPs: Strong Treaty Protection

### What Is an RRSP?

A Registered Retirement Savings Plan (RRSP) is a Canadian retirement account. Contributions, growth, withdrawals, and conversion rules follow Canadian law and the account terms. The U.S. analysis follows the treaty, Rev. Proc. 2014-55, and current filing instructions rather than the account label alone.

### US Tax Treatment Under the Treaty

The **US-Canada tax treaty (Article XVIII)** provides strong protection:

1. **Deferral:** The treaty can defer U.S. taxation of qualifying income inside an RRSP or RRIF until distribution.
2. **Foreign-trust reporting exception:** The current Form 3520 instructions reference the Rev. Proc. 2014-55 exception for qualifying Canadian retirement plans.
3. **Distributions:** Withdrawals still require U.S. income, withholding, treaty, and foreign-tax-credit analysis.
4. **Account changes:** A conversion, rollover, inherited account, or nonstandard plan may require a separate review.

### Form 8891 (Obsolete)

Historically, taxpayers filed **Form 8891** to elect treaty deferral. The IRS simplified this in 2014 — now you claim the treaty benefit directly on Form 1040 without a separate election form.

### Form 3520: Check the exception

The current Form 3520 instructions expressly list qualifying Canadian RRSPs, RRIFs, and certain other Canadian retirement plans within an exception. Confirm the account and transaction fit the cited authority; do not assume every Canadian savings or investment account qualifies.

## Canadian TFSAs: A separate classification question

### What Is a TFSA?

A Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA) is a Canadian account whose contributions, investment choices, and withdrawals follow Canadian law and the account agreement. It is not the same account type as an RRSP or RRIF for U.S. reporting analysis.

### U.S. tax treatment: do not assume the label controls

The RRSP/RRIF exception does not automatically extend to a TFSA. But the absence of that exception does not by itself prove foreign-trust treatment or a particular form. Review the account's legal structure, ownership rights, contributions, distributions, and underlying holdings.

### Why TFSAs Are Particularly Bad for US Taxpayers

1. **Deferral mismatch risk:** Canadian tax treatment does not automatically create U.S. tax deferral.
2. **Classification risk:** The account may require trust, asset, treaty, or other reporting analysis.
3. **PFIC risk:** A Canadian mutual fund or ETF may be a PFIC, but the form requirement depends on the specific holding and ownership path.
4. **Recordkeeping:** Contributions, distributions, basis, income, and account statements matter.

### Before contributing, transferring, or closing an account

Do not treat this page as an investment recommendation. Compare the account's Canadian purpose, U.S. tax result, treaty position, holdings, liquidity, and recordkeeping with qualified cross-border advice before changing the account.

## RRIFs and Annuities

When an RRSP is converted to a RRIF or annuity, analyze the conversion under the account terms, treaty, Rev. Proc. 2014-55, and current filing instructions. Distributions can be taxable in both countries, and withholding and foreign-tax-credit treatment require year-specific calculations.

## Common Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Treating a TFSA like an RRSP

The accounts have different Canadian and U.S. rules. A TFSA needs its own classification and holdings review.

### Mistake 2: Holding Canadian Mutual Funds in an RRSP

An RRSP's treaty and Form 3520 treatment does not automatically answer whether a particular underlying holding is a PFIC or whether another form applies. Test the holding, ownership path, and exception separately.

### Mistake 3: Not Reporting RRSP Distributions

Some Americans assume the treaty eliminates all US tax on RRSPs. It does not — it only **defers** tax until distribution. When you withdraw, you must report the distribution on your US return.
