An IRS notice received at a foreign address creates two problems at once: the tax question and the logistics of responding from abroad. The first step is not to panic or immediately amend a return. Identify the notice, preserve the response date, compare the claim to the filed records, and decide whether the issue is a straightforward correction, a missing filing, or a matter that needs specialist review.

The [IRS notice guide for expats](/guides/irs-notices-expats) is a starting point. The exact letter controls. A CP2000, a balance-due notice, an identity-verification request, an FBAR-related contact, and a foreign bank FATCA letter are not interchangeable. Use the notice number, tax year, issuing agency, and response instructions to classify the matter.

## Build a notice cover sheet

Create a one-page cover sheet before working through the details. Record the notice type, tax year, date on the letter, date received, response deadline, reply address, upload or fax option, payment instructions, and any telephone number. Add the taxpayer name, the last four digits or internal file reference, and whether the notice is addressed to one spouse, both spouses, an entity, or a prior address.

Scan the complete notice, including the back page and inserts. Keep the envelope if the postmark or international delivery date could matter. Do not rely on a photograph of the first page. The response instructions often differ depending on whether the taxpayer agrees, disagrees, needs a correction, or has already sent a document.

If the letter arrived at an old address, update the IRS address through the appropriate process and preserve evidence of the address change. An address update does not extend a response deadline. If the taxpayer has an authorized representative, confirm whether the authorization covers the tax year and notice issue.

## CP2000: reconcile the information match

A CP2000 usually says that third-party information does not match the filed return. Create a row for every proposed item: payer, form type, amount, tax year, taxpayer identification number, and the line or schedule where the item appeared—or should have appeared—on the return.

For wages and contractor payments, compare the W-2 or 1099 with payroll records, invoices, bank deposits, and the work-location calendar. For interest and dividends, compare the payer statement with the year-end account statements and the account inventory. For pensions, compare the distribution statement with the local plan records, treaty workpaper, and the Form 1040 treatment.

Americans abroad should check currency conversion, duplicates, corrected forms, joint ownership, and tax-year timing. A foreign institution may issue a local statement based on a different year or report an amount that was later corrected. A U.S. payer may use an old address or identification number. These facts do not make the notice disappear; they explain what evidence belongs in the response.

If the information is correct, review connected calculations before agreeing. Additional income may change the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion, foreign tax credit limitation, self-employment tax, retirement contribution, premium credit, or state filing. If the information is wrong, explain the mismatch item by item and attach records that answer the proposal.

The [CP2000 article](/blog/cp2000-expat-what-to-do) gives a shorter comparison checklist. Do not file an amended return automatically. Follow the notice’s instructions about whether an amended return, a written response, or both are required.

## FATCA and foreign-bank letters

A FATCA letter from a foreign bank is often a documentation request rather than an IRS tax assessment. The bank may ask whether the customer is a U.S. citizen or resident, request a U.S. tax identification number, ask for a tax form, or seek confirmation of the account holder’s tax residency. The exact request and the bank’s deadline matter.

Read the bank letter alongside the account-opening records. Identify the legal account holder, joint owners, beneficial owners, authorized signers, entity classification, and country of residence. If the bank’s records are wrong, ask what correction process it provides and retain the submission confirmation. If the account belongs to an entity or trust, do not answer as an individual without reviewing the governing documents.

A bank’s FATCA question does not replace U.S. filing analysis. The account may need to be considered for FBAR, Form 8938, Schedule B, Form 3520, Form 5471, Form 8865, or another form. The [FBAR guide](/guides/fbar-guide) explains the account inventory; the [FinCEN FBAR guidance](https://www.fincen.gov/report-foreign-bank-and-financial-accounts) explains the agency’s filing channel. The bank’s request and the U.S. filing map should agree, but they serve different purposes.

Do not send an SSN, passport scan, account password, or full tax return by ordinary email or WhatsApp unless you have confirmed a secure channel and the recipient is authorized. Use the bank’s secure portal where available. For a tax review, redact preliminary messages and share sensitive records only through the approved intake process.

## Other notice types to classify

An IRS balance-due notice requires a payment and account reconciliation review. Confirm whether the amount reflects a return, an amended return, a prior notice, interest, a penalty, or a payment that was not credited. A payment made from abroad can take time to post, so preserve the payment confirmation and bank reference.

An identity-verification notice is different from a substantive audit. Follow the current identity instructions and do not substitute a tax argument for the requested verification. If the taxpayer cannot complete an online step from abroad, document the problem and use the alternative method described by the notice or current IRS guidance.

A notice about missing information returns may involve an FBAR, Form 8938, Form 3520, Form 5471, Form 8621, or another form. Identify the form, year, owner, account or entity, and reason it was omitted. Do not choose a catch-up path based solely on the notice. Check filing history, residence, willfulness facts, taxes due, and the current IRS procedures before filing.

State notices require a separate analysis. A federal notice may be shared with a state agency, but a state residency or sourcing conclusion does not automatically follow. Review the former state’s domicile rules, the departure-year return, the source schedule, and the notice deadline. The [state tax guide](/guides/state-taxes-abroad) explains why a foreign home is not by itself proof that a state obligation ended.

## Responding from abroad

Prepare the response in the order the notice presents the issues. Start with a short conclusion, then a numbered answer to each proposed change, then an indexed exhibit list. Put the taxpayer name, notice number, tax year, and page number on every exhibit. Use copies, not irreplaceable originals, unless the agency specifically requires otherwise.

Translate only what needs translation, preserve the original, and explain the currency conversion method. If the amount changed because of exchange rates, show the source rate, date, and calculation. If the notice concerns a foreign pension or entity, include the plan or formation document that supports the classification, not only a bank statement.

Reconcile the response to the filed return and any amended return. The explanation, numbers, and schedules should tell one story. If the taxpayer agrees with one item and disagrees with another, separate the positions. If a document is unavailable, say what was requested, what substitute evidence is attached, and when the original is expected.

Send the response using the method in the notice. Keep delivery proof, upload confirmation, fax report, mailing receipt, and the final PDF. Set a follow-up date that is earlier than the next expected deadline. The IRS may take time to process a response; a follow-up file prevents the taxpayer from losing the thread.

## When not to self-manage

Get professional review before responding when the notice involves multiple unfiled years, a foreign corporation or trust, PFICs, a willfulness question, a passport restriction, a levy or lien, an appeal, litigation, a criminal concern, or a state domicile dispute. An educational checklist cannot determine legal representation boundaries.

FileAbroad can review a notice and organize a return-preparation response when the issue fits the agreed service scope. A paid consultation comes first so the notice, response date, records, and requested outcome can be assessed. The written scope should identify the tax years, notices, documents, deliverable, assumptions, and any issues that require an attorney or another specialist.

## A response packet that an IRS reviewer can follow

Use a consistent packet order: cover letter, copy of the notice, summary of the requested result, numbered response to each issue, corrected schedules or forms, supporting records, translation notes, and proof of timely submission. Put a table of contents at the front. If the response contains foreign statements, explain the currency, tax year, and relevant line instead of attaching a large unindexed export.

A short response is often easier to review than a long narrative. For each issue, state whether the taxpayer agrees, disagrees, or needs to correct the record. Name the attached exhibit that proves the point. If the matter involves a corrected payer statement, include the corrected statement and explain what changed. If income was already reported under another form, show the filed line and schedule.

Keep a separate internal workpaper for unresolved questions. Do not bury a classification problem inside the response. A question about whether an account is a trust, whether a pension qualifies for treaty treatment, or whether a missing information return belongs in a catch-up procedure may require a different engagement or an attorney. The response can acknowledge the boundary without guessing.

## Protecting the next filing year

After responding, update the annual tax checklist. Add the notice type, the reason it arose, the record that would have prevented it, and the control for the next year. For example, a CP2000 caused by a corrected brokerage statement may require a quarterly statement review; a FATCA letter may reveal an account omitted from the inventory; a state notice may show that an old address remained on a form.

Do not assume that resolving one notice closes every related issue. A CP2000 may change federal tax but leave FBAR and Form 8938 questions untouched. A bank’s FATCA documentation may correct the account record but not report taxable interest. A state notice may need a separate return or domicile explanation. Reconcile the response outcome to the next return, account inventory, and calendar.

If the IRS sends a follow-up, attach the previous response and processing proof. Record the date and result of every call or upload. An organized history helps a professional understand the case quickly and protects the taxpayer from sending inconsistent explanations.

For a taxpayer outside the United States, designate one secure place for the notice file and one calendar owner. Store the original notice, response draft, final response, exhibits, delivery confirmation, and later correspondence under the tax year. If a spouse or adviser is helping, record who is authorized to speak and which documents were shared. This simple chain of custody matters when the response spans several time zones, an old address, or more than one agency.

If the notice changes the federal return, update the state return and foreign-tax workpaper review list even when no state or foreign amendment is ultimately required. If a bank letter reveals a previously unknown account, add it to the next FBAR and Form 8938 inventory review. The response is complete only when the underlying control is updated for the next filing year.

## Notice triage after the first response

Once the first response is sent, keep the case open until the agency’s processing result is matched to the original issue. A notice can be generated again because a document was not attached, a payment posted to a different period, a corrected information return arrived later, or the agency processed only part of the response. Compare every follow-up to the original notice number, tax year, and proposed amount before drafting a new explanation.

Create a status table with the date of each letter, the action taken, the delivery proof, the amount at issue, and the next expected agency action. If the taxpayer called, preserve the date, department, representative reference, and factual summary of the call. A phone conversation is not a substitute for the written record, but it can explain why a follow-up was expected or why the agency requested a particular exhibit.

Separate response work from payment planning. If the notice shows a balance, confirm whether the taxpayer agrees with the underlying tax, the penalty, the interest, or none of them. A payment arrangement or payment made from abroad may address collection while a separate response or appeal addresses the calculation. Do not treat a payment as an admission unless the taxpayer and qualified adviser have reviewed that consequence.

When the notice touches several years, build a cross-year issue map. Show the original return, the information match, the response, the processing result, and the remaining open question for each year. This helps prevent an explanation for one year from accidentally being copied into another year with different residence dates, ownership, currency records, or filing positions.

Finally, convert the resolved issue into a control for the next filing cycle. Add a statement-reconciliation date, account-inventory owner, secure-document location, and deadline reminder. The goal is not merely to close a letter; it is to preserve the evidence and process that make the next filing easier to defend.

## Official sources

When the notice concerns a foreign payer or account, add the payer’s legal name, country, account or policy number, and the exact statement period to the response index. This gives the reviewer a way to distinguish a corrected report from a duplicate report and helps a later preparer reconcile the same item without reopening the entire file.

Also record whether the taxpayer responded personally, through an authorized representative, or through a secure agency portal. That small administrative fact can explain later correspondence and prevents two people from sending conflicting replies.

Keep the response history with the return history so a future review sees the notice, the evidence, and the filing position together.
