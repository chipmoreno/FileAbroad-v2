# State Taxes for Americans Abroad: Domicile, Residency, and Filing

Federal filing is only one layer of an expat’s compliance picture. A person can live outside the United States, qualify for federal expat provisions, and still have a state-tax filing question because a state treats the person as a resident, domiciliary, or taxpayer with state-source income.

This guide is a planning framework, not a state residency opinion. State rules change, and the facts that matter are different from one state to another.

## Start with three separate questions

### 1. Did you end your old domicile?

Domicile is the place you intend to make your permanent home. A foreign address is useful evidence, but it is not always decisive. A state may examine your conduct, family and property ties, voter registration, driver's license, business activity, and statements of intent.

### 2. Did you become a resident under that state’s tests?

States may use day-count rules, domicile rules, statutory residency tests, or combinations of them. Some states have special safe harbors; others focus heavily on whether you maintained a permanent place of abode or continued to treat the state as home.

### 3. Did you earn state-source income after leaving?

Even a nonresident may have a state filing obligation for income sourced to that state. Examples can include work physically performed there, rental income from property there, or business activity tied to the state. The source rules depend on the income type and state law.

## The departure file to build

Create a dated record rather than relying on memory years later:

- foreign lease, residence permit, utility records, and travel history;
- move date, shipping records, and the date your foreign home became available;
- surrender or change of driver's license, voter registration, vehicle registration, and mailing address;
- sale or rental of former U.S. housing and the date it stopped being available for personal use;
- spouse and dependent residence, employment, school, and household facts;
- foreign employment or business records and any continuing U.S. workdays;
- copies of prior state returns and any state correspondence.

Do not make a filing position depend on one document. The complete timeline is more useful than a single declaration.

## State-specific review matters

California, Virginia, New York, New Mexico, South Carolina, and Massachusetts each require a state-specific review. The practical question is not simply whether a state has an income tax. It is whether your facts satisfy that state’s residency, domicile, or source-income rules for the year.

Use the dedicated pages for [California](/state-taxes/california), [Virginia](/state-taxes/virginia), [New York](/state-taxes/new-york), [New Mexico](/state-taxes/new-mexico), [South Carolina](/state-taxes/south-carolina), and [Massachusetts](/state-taxes/massachusetts) as starting points. States without a broad personal income tax still deserve a check for other filing, business, property, or source-income issues.

## How federal and state filings interact

The federal return may contain the facts needed to prepare a state return, but federal exclusions and credits do not automatically remove a state obligation. State conformity to federal income, foreign tax credits, retirement income, and foreign earned income rules varies.

Keep the federal and state workpapers consistent. If your state position depends on a departure date, domicile change, or allocation of workdays, document that position separately rather than assuming the federal return answers it.

## When to obtain specialist help

Escalate before filing when the case involves a former state audit, an active state notice, a retained home, a spouse or dependents in the state, a business or professional license, large investment or property income, or a dispute over domicile. A tax preparer can prepare an accepted return within scope; a contested residency matter may require state counsel or another credentialed specialist.

For current federal filing context, review the [IRS guidance for U.S. citizens and residents abroad](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/us-citizens-and-residents-abroad-filing-requirements) and the [IRS Publication 54](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p54). For a preliminary FileAbroad scope review, book a [consultation](/consultation) and describe your last state, departure date, continuing ties, and current country.
