# U.S. Expats Married to Foreign Spouses: Filing and Reporting Guide

Marriage to a non-U.S. spouse creates a filing-status decision, not an automatic filing strategy. The spouse’s residency status, identification, worldwide income, foreign accounts, community-property law, and the couple’s goals should be mapped before choosing joint or separate filing.

## Joint filing and the resident-spouse election

A U.S. citizen or resident may elect to treat a nonresident alien spouse as a U.S. resident for the tax year and file jointly. This can open or change credits and tax brackets, but it generally brings the spouse’s worldwide income into the U.S. return and creates an ongoing information and recordkeeping obligation.

Read the election language and consent requirements in the current Form 1040 instructions. Keep the marriage certificate, identification documents, foreign income records, and signed statements used for the election.

## Separate filing is not always simpler

Married filing separately may avoid the resident-spouse election, but it can alter standard deductions, credits, IRA eligibility, community-property allocation, and the reporting of jointly held assets. A separate return still requires a complete review of the U.S. taxpayer’s worldwide income and foreign accounts.

## Foreign-spouse accounts and assets

Review accounts where the U.S. spouse has financial interest, joint ownership, signature authority, or other access. The FBAR and Form 8938 rules are not identical. A foreign spouse’s account may be outside the U.S. taxpayer’s filing if the facts and exceptions support that result, but a blanket “my spouse owns it” assumption is unsafe.

## Foreign income and currency

Collect the spouse’s wages, business income, pensions, interest, dividends, gains, rental income, and local tax returns when a joint return includes worldwide income. Convert amounts using a documented method and keep the local records. Foreign tax credits may be available, but source, basket, and creditability rules still apply.

## Children, gifts, and inherited accounts

Birth or adoption certificates, foreign education accounts, gifts, inheritances, and trusts can introduce additional reporting. Review Form 3520, Form 8938, FBAR, and any treaty or community-property issue separately.
