# U.S. Tax Treaties for Americans Abroad

An income-tax treaty is a coordination agreement between the United States and another country. It may affect who can tax a category of income, the rate applied to certain payments, residence tie-breakers, pension treatment, or relief from double taxation. It does not turn the U.S. tax return into an optional filing.

## Read the treaty in the right order

Start with the country’s treaty and protocol, then identify:

1. the residence article and any tie-breaker rules;
2. the article for the income type—employment, business, pensions, interest, dividends, royalties, or capital gains;
3. the saving clause, which often preserves U.S. taxing rights over citizens;
4. limitations, notification requirements, and any competent-authority procedure;
5. the U.S. form or disclosure needed to claim the position.

The [IRS tax treaty tables](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/tax-treaties) and the [IRS treaty documents index](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/international-businesses/united-states-income-tax-treaties-a-to-z) are the right starting points. Use the current treaty text, not a summary copied from a relocation blog.

## Treaty benefits are income-specific

A treaty may treat pension income differently from employment income, and interest differently from dividends. A benefit can depend on the recipient’s residence, the payer, the source of the services, the place of work, or whether the saving clause applies.

Foreign tax credits are a separate mechanism. A treaty may reduce foreign withholding or allocate taxing rights, while the Foreign Tax Credit may address income tax actually paid or accrued. Do not claim both benefits on the same tax without checking the limitation rules.

## Form 8833 and disclosure

If you take a treaty-based return position that overrides or modifies the Internal Revenue Code, review the [Form 8833 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8833). The filing position should identify the treaty, article, code provision affected, facts, and required disclosure. Some treaty benefits are claimed on other forms or schedules, and some positions may be unavailable because of the saving clause.

FileAbroad’s [Form 8833 page](/forms/8833-treaty-benefits) is an educational overview. It is not a legal opinion on a treaty position. Treaty interpretation, competent-authority matters, residency disputes, and aggressive positions may require a credentialed reviewer or referral.

## Common treaty mistakes

- treating a visa or local tax-residence certificate as conclusive for U.S. purposes;
- ignoring the saving clause for U.S. citizens;
- assuming a treaty eliminates FBAR, FATCA, Form 3520, Form 8621, or Form 5471;
- relying on an outdated treaty summary instead of the current text and protocol;
- claiming a reduced withholding rate without giving the payer the required form;
- using a treaty position without documenting the article and facts.
